# Nodes rebalance passively: inventory pricing, a rate feed and market makers

**Areas:** [Connector](../areas/connector.md#inventory-pricing), [Rate feed](../areas/rate-feed.md), [Settlement](../areas/settlement.md#credit).

## Facts

- A core node that converts between two ledgers drifts. Every conversion takes in one asset and pays out the other, so a node through which more flows one way fills up with one and runs out of the other. Once its owner holds nothing on a ledger, the owner's group limit refuses every conversion out of it (T04), and nothing moves the node back.
- A node holds its assets on ledgers it settles on, and only peers can move them in its books: it cannot trade on its own without an exchange account, a ledger's decentralised exchange, or keys and logic for each of them, and every one of those is a new way to lose the owner's funds.
- Peers settle only over settlement hubs ([hubs-only](hubs-only.md)), so a node holds each asset it settles in on two ledgers: its account in the hub, which packets travel on, and the owner book on the asset's own ledger that funds it, which peers with nobody ([Settlement](../areas/settlement.md#owner-books)). The node moves funds between the two by itself, keeping the hub between a floor and a ceiling ([Settlement hubs](../areas/hubs.md#in-the-node)), so where an asset sits between them says how the node funds its hub, not how much of the asset it holds.
- Automated market makers on layer-1 ledgers rebalance passively: their price follows their inventory, so arbitrageurs, who profit from any gap to the market, bring them back. Their price is their inventory alone, though, so an arbitrageur can push them as far as the market moves, and they trust no oracle.
- A node already has an oracle: its spot rate, the median of three independent sources ([exchange-rates](exchange-rates.md)). One wrong source cannot move it past what the other two say.
- Peering is one per node pair, on one scheme, chosen automatically, and the owner's own endpoints never convert ([owner-ledger](owner-ledger.md)). One node that wanted to trade through a core node in both directions would need two peerings with it, on two schemes.
- An arbitrageur needs every core node's prices, often and cheaply; a node that answers price requests over HTTPS pays a handshake per asker and per poll.

## Decision

- **Each core node prices conversions by its inventory**, anchored at spot. For each asset that converts, the node values the owner's funds of it at spot; `s_i` is its share of the total, `t_i` its target share. A unit of the asset, on any of its ledgers, is worth `w_i = (1 − k) + k · t_i / s_i` against spot, and transit from `a` to `b` converts at `spot · w_a / w_b · (1 − spread)`. Bringing in what the node lacks gets better than spot; taking it out gets worse. So whoever profits from the gap, a market maker or an ordinary payment, moves the node back toward its targets, and the node itself never trades.
- **Each asset counts whole.** A settlement hub's ledger and the owner book that funds the hub are one holding, with one share and one target: the curve follows what the owner holds of the asset, not where the node's own moves left it. A conversion between two ledgers of one asset is at spot less the spread. An owner book that funds no hub the node prices counts as an asset of its own, as does any other ledger. Out of a ledger the owner holds nothing on, nothing converts, however much of its asset is on the other: the node cannot pay out there.
- **Each packet pays its own impact, priced at the worst state the packets in flight can leave.** A packet from `a` to `b` is priced with every ledger of `a`'s asset holding what is posted and every pending credit, but no pending debit, and `a` itself the packet's own amount too; and with every ledger of `b`'s asset holding what is posted less every pending debit, but no pending credit, and `b` itself less what the packet takes out; every other ledger holds what is posted. As on an AMM, a packet's price includes its own move, so a round trip pays both ways and never gains. Pricing at the worst state in flight means no held, rejected or concurrent packet moves another's price in its favour: a peer that holds Prepares out of a ledger to make it look scarce, converts its own packets at the shifted price and then rejects the held ones, gains nothing, and neither does one who splits a trip into concurrent legs.
- **The bonus is capped at `1 + k`; the penalty is not.** However skewed the node, it never pays more than `k` above spot for what it lacks, so a market whose price differs from the node's oracle by less than `k` cannot drain it; what the node lacks gets dearer the scarcer it is, and nothing converts out of a ledger the owner holds too little on.
- **The parameters are starting points.** `k` is an owner setting beside the spread, on by default at 5%, and the targets are equal shares by value, one per asset. At 5% a 25/75 split costs about 6% on the way out of the scarce asset, a 5/95 split about a third. Both are to be tuned against what the network shows, and targets may become owner settings.
- **A price table says the most a packet gets.** It is rebuilt whenever the rates, the owner's funds or the settings change: for each ordered pair of ledgers with fresh rates, the rate of a vanishingly small packet at the worst state in flight, in exact integer arithmetic, rounded down. A real packet is priced by the same curve at its own state, which the node reads in constant time per ledger, and gets that rate or less. What a node publishes is that bound. Conversions into the owner's own endpoints stay at spot, uncharged: the owner's own payments do not pay the owner.
- **A public rate feed, over UDP.** A core node publishes its table as one signed datagram, generated and signed once and sent to every subscriber, with no retries and no state beyond a bounded list of subscribers. A subscriber proves its address with a cookie the node derives from a boot secret and the address before it gets anything larger than its request, so a forged source address cannot aim the feed at a victim. Signatures are by the node key under an OID of their own, verified by ZIP-215 as everything else.
- **Market makers are periphery nodes.** A market maker runs two periphery nodes, each on one scheme and pinned to the core node it trades with: one pays the core node on one ledger, the other is paid on the other, and the market maker rebalances its own side on a ledger's exchange. That fits one peering per node pair, needs no change to how peers convert, and keeps the market maker's keys on its own nodes.

## Accepted

- **The spread's gain can be negative.** The curve moves the rate past spot in the sender's favour, so a node short of an asset pays for getting it back. Like the spread, this is not booked per packet: it is the net of the FX accounts.
- **A node may pay up to `k` above spot** for what it lacks, which an arbitrageur takes when the market is that far from the oracle. That is the price of rebalancing, bounded by `k` per unit.
- **The oracle's errors pass through.** A spot rate off by less than `k` from the market leaves a node's curve exploitable by that much; the median of three sources keeps it close.
- **The feed says the most a packet gets, not exactly what it gets.** A packet gets less than the published rate by its own impact, near target about `4k` times its share of the node's value, and by whatever moved since the table was built. A subscriber that knows the node's value and `k` can work out the rest; one that does not still never gets more than it was told.
- **A packet pays for what is in flight against it.** Pricing at the worst state charges a packet for packets in flight that may yet be rejected, so a burst in one direction prices each packet as if the ones before it were fulfilled, and a peer that holds Prepares can make others' prices worse, though never better. That costs the holder its in-flight room and gains it nothing.
- **A ledger whose rate is stale is left out**, so its pairs convert nothing, as before; a pair of ledgers in the same currency now needs that currency's rate too, since the curve values holdings across all of them.
- **An owner book is priced as a ledger,** though no peer's packet travels on it. Its spot rate is what a peer's packet into the owner's own endpoints on it converts at, and the pairs it is in, which a feed names, are what the curve says and are never taken in transit.
- **Where an asset sits does not move its price.** Counted whole, a hub that holds little of an asset the node holds enough of takes it in and pays it out at the asset's weight until nothing is left on the hub to pay out; refilling the hub is for the node's own moves, not the curve.

## Rejected

- *The node trading on a ledger's exchange or an exchange account:* keys and logic per venue, slippage and counterparty risk on the owner's funds, and a node that acts on its own rather than on what its peers bring.
- *Pricing each ledger apart, a hub's ledger apart from the owner book that funds it:* the node's own moves, which keep the hub between its floor and ceiling, would set the curve. With most of an asset in its book, its hub reads short, and the node pays the bonus for an asset it holds plenty of, and charges for the one it lacks; its feed then sends market makers' trades the wrong way, further from balance.
- *A pure AMM curve, priced by the inventory alone:* without the oracle, an arbitrageur could move a node's price as far as the market moves, and the node would follow any market, a manipulated one included.
- *An uncapped bonus:* a node with nothing left of an asset would pay without bound to get it back, and a single trade could take the rest of its funds.
- *Pricing at the table, before a packet's own move:* bringing an asset in and taking it straight back out after the table is rebuilt gains the price's move in between, about `4k · Δ² / V` per trip for `Δ` moved on a node of value `V`, without bound over time; and a table built from what the owner may spend, pending debits less, let a peer hold Prepares out of a ledger to shift the price, convert at it, and reject the held ones for free.
- *A margin of one in-flight cap on every price:* pricing every pair as if a cap's worth had already moved against it would bound a trip's gain, but charges every packet, however small, the impact of the largest burst, and still lets a trip larger than the margin gain.
- *A default spread:* a spread larger than a trip's gain removes it for packets up to some size, but taxes every conversion, and a node's value and its peers' packet sizes change, so no one spread is large enough without being too large.
- *Accepting the drain:* a round-tripper takes a little per trip, as fast as the in-flight limits allow, from every node that converts, with nothing to show for it; held Prepares double it, and cost the holder nothing.
- *Rates over HTTPS on request:* a handshake per asker and per poll, and state per connection, for data that is the same for everyone.
- *A feed without proof of address:* a reply larger than the request, sent to any source address, is a reflection amplifier.
- *One market-maker node trading through a core node both ways:* it would need two peerings with the same node, on two schemes, and the owner's own endpoints never convert.
