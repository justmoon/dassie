Exchange rates

A payment can come in as XRP and leave as dollars, or the other way. This page explains where your node's rates come from, how it prices a conversion, and how it keeps its holdings in balance without ever trading on its own.

One rate that matters

Every ledger a node settles on today holds either XRP or a dollar token, such as DSUSD on the testnet. A dollar token is booked as dollars, one to one. So only one rate ever moves money: the price of XRP in dollars.

Your node keeps rates for a few more currencies (euros, pounds, yen, Swiss francs, Canadian and Australian dollars), but only to show you your total in the currency you choose. A total is shown only while every rate it needs is fresh. With one missing, the wallet shows no total rather than a wrong one.

Where rates come from

Every minute, your node asks its rate sources and takes the median of the answers. A new node starts with four sources, so that every currency is covered three times, from three independent places:

Source Covers
Coinbase's exchange rates every currency
Kraken's ticker every currency
Bitstamp's ticker XRP
The European Central Bank's daily reference rates the other currencies
  • The median. A rate counts once more than half of its sources answered. One wrong source can move it only as far as the others allow. With one source silent, the other two must agree closely.
  • Exact. Rates are read and used as exact decimals, never as floating point, and every conversion rounds down.
  • Fresh, or nothing. Your node keeps its last rates across a restart. A rate older than an hour is not used: until a fresh one arrives, payments that would convert through it are turned away with a temporary error, and payments that stay on one ledger go on as usual.

You can change the list of sources in the wallet. See Exchange-rate settings.

The exchanges license their data for personal or internal use only. Sources whose terms allow a node to convert by them for a fee, XRP's price from the XRP Ledger's own XRP/RLUSD pool, and the other currencies from four central banks in place of the exchanges, are not there yet. What's next

Dollar tokens and the peg guard

Booking a dollar token at par is only safe while the token is worth a dollar. A node that converted at par while the token was off its peg could be drained. So each dollar token has a peg guard: once a minute your node reads the token's pool on the XRP Ledger, with a proof, and prices the token against XRP's rate.

When the token stays off its peg for a while, the guard pauses every conversion to or from it, on your own account and in its hub alike. Settling and paying out in the token go on. When the token is back near a dollar for a longer while, conversions start again. A guard that cannot read the pool for long enough pauses too, rather than guess. See Dollar tokens and the peg guard for what you see in the wallet.

Pricing by what you hold

A node that converts for others drifts. If more payments flow from XRP to dollars through it than back, it fills up with XRP and runs out of dollars. Once it holds nothing on a ledger, it can convert nothing out of it. Your node never trades on an exchange to fix this. Instead, it lets its price lean.

  • A target for each asset. Your node values what you hold of each asset at the market rate, and aims for equal shares, one per asset. What it holds in a hub and on your own account on the ledger count together, as one holding.
  • Better for what it lacks, worse for what it has. Bringing in an asset your node is short of gets a better rate than the market; taking it out gets a worse one. So whoever profits from the gap, a market maker or an ordinary payment, moves your node back toward balance.
  • A cap on the bonus, none on the penalty. Your node never pays more than the rebalancing share above the market for what it lacks, 5% by default. Taking out what it is short of gets dearer the scarcer it is, and nothing converts out of a ledger you hold nothing on.
  • Each packet pays its own way. A packet is priced after its own effect on your holdings, and as if every other packet in flight against it went through. So a round trip never gains, and nobody can improve their price by holding other payments back.

With the default 5%, a node that holds a quarter of its value in one asset and three quarters in the other pays the scarce one out at about 6% below the market rate.

Three settings in the wallet shape this, all charged only on what passes from one peer to another:

Setting What it does Default
Fee A share of what passes through, rounded down. 0%
Spread A share of what is converted from one currency to another. 0%
Rebalancing How far your holdings lean the rate: the bonus cap above. 5%

Your own payments are never charged, and when a peer pays you in another currency, it converts at the market rate. A packet only converts when it has to: your node sends it on in the asset it came in, if any next hop can take that asset.

The rate feed

A market maker needs to know every node's rates, often and cheaply. So each core node publishes them as a rate feed: one small signed message, sent over UDP to everyone who subscribed. It says, for each pair of hubs your peers can pay you over, the most a packet gets, with your fee and the largest packet you take.

  • Your node publishes at most once a second while its rates change, and every thirty seconds anyway, so subscribers know it is alive.
  • A subscriber first proves it receives at its address, so nobody can aim the feed at someone else.
  • The feed listens on UDP port 7768. If the node cannot bind that port, it runs as usual without a feed. Open UDP 7768 in your firewall so subscribers can reach it. A periphery node publishes none.
  • dassie status and the wallet's network page show whether the feed is served and how many subscribe.

To read a feed yourself, see Read a rate feed and the wire format.

Market makers

A market maker watches the feeds of the core nodes it trades through and an AMM pool on the XRP Ledger. When a node's feed and the pool disagree by more than a margin, it pays through that node in the asset the node lacks, takes the other asset out, and evens out its own side on the pool. Each such trade moves the node back toward balance, and the market maker keeps the difference.

It runs as dassie market-maker, with two periphery nodes of its own, one in each asset's hub, both pinned to the core nodes it trades through. It holds no books: every amount is moved by a node or by the ledger. See Run a market maker.
